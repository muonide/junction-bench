Model, equations and further reading

This section documents what the solver computes on this tab, in roughly the order a textbook chapter on metal–semiconductor contacts would present it. Equation numbers are local to the tab. Material constants are room-temperature (300 K) literature values (Sze & Ng; the Ioffe database) and are approximate.

1 · Energy bands before contact

Each material is placed on a common vacuum level. A metal is described by its work function φm. A semiconductor is described by its electron affinity χ (vacuum level to EC), band gap Eg, and the position of its Fermi level, which together give the semiconductor work function

qφs = qχ + (EC − EF)(1)

Fig. 1 draws exactly these quantities. The difference φm − φs is the contact potential that must be absorbed once the two materials share a Fermi level.

2 · Carrier statistics in the bulk

EF is found from charge neutrality, p − n + ND+ − NA− = 0, using Fermi–Dirac statistics for the free carriers,

n = NC F1/2(ηn),   ηn = (EF − EC)/kT(2)

where F1/2 is the normalized Fermi–Dirac integral (evaluated with the Aymerich-Humet approximation, ≈0.5 % accuracy). For ηn ≪ −1 this reduces to the Boltzmann form n = NCeηn. Dopants ionize incompletely,

ND+ = ND1 + gD e(EF − ED)/kT,   NA− = NA1 + gA e(EA − EF)/kT(3)

with degeneracy factors gD = 2 and gA = 4. At high concentration the impurity wavefunctions overlap and the ionization energy shrinks. The model uses ED(N) = ED(0)[1 − (N/NM)1/3], with full ionization above the Mott density given by NM1/3aB* ≈ 0.26 (Edwards & Sienko), where aB* = εr(m0/m*)·0.0529 nm is the effective Bohr radius. That is why P in Si is fully ionized at 10¹⁹ cm⁻³ while Mg in GaN stays mostly neutral.

3 · Barrier height

In the Schottky–Mott limit the barrier seen by electrons in the metal is set by the vacuum-level alignment,

qφBn = qφm − qχ,   qφBp = Eg + qχ − qφm(4)

Real interfaces carry states in the gap that exchange charge with the metal and pin EF near a charge-neutrality level φ0 (measured from EV). The Cowley–Sze interpolation used here is

qφBn = S(qφm − qχ) + (1 − S)(Eg − qφ0),   S = 11 + q²δDit/εi(5)

S = 1 recovers Schottky–Mott and S → 0 is the Bardeen limit. When S < 1 the vacuum level steps at the interface (an interface dipole of thickness δ), and the metal sheet charge in Fig. 3 includes the interface-state charge. Measured S is ≈0.1 for covalent semiconductors (Si, GaAs) and approaches 1 for ionic ones. Tung's review discusses the competing microscopic explanations: metal-induced gap states, defects and interface bonding.

4 · Electrostatics

In one dimension Poisson's equation, d²ψ/dx² = −ρ(ψ)/εs, has the exact first integral

ℰ²(ψ) = 2εs∫0ψ ρ(ψ′) dψ′(6)

which the tab evaluates with Fermi–Dirac statistics (using F3/2, whose derivative is F1/2) and band-bending-dependent dopant ionization. No depletion approximation is made. Integrating dx = dψ/ℰ(ψ) from the interface into the bulk gives the band profile in Fig. 2, and Gauss's law gives the sheet charge σm = −εsℰs. For orientation, the readouts also give the depletion-approximation width and the extrinsic Debye length,

W = (2εs(Vbi − V − kT/q)qN)1/2,   LD = (εskTq²N)1/2(7)

W describes depletion; LD sets the thickness of accumulation layers. By default minority carriers are held at their bulk density, the usual Schottky-diode picture. With the minority-carrier option on, they follow equilibrium statistics at zero bias, which can produce an inversion layer under large barriers. Under bias the majority quasi-Fermi level is taken as flat through the depletion region.

5 · Current transport

For a nondegenerate semiconductor and a wide barrier, current is limited by thermionic emission over the barrier top,

J = A**T² e−qφB/kT(eqV/kT − 1),   A** = 4πqm*k²h³(8)

with effective Richardson constants of 112 A cm⁻² K⁻² (n-Si), 32 (p-Si), 8.2 (n-GaAs) and 26 (n-GaN). The image force lowers the barrier by

Δφ = √qℰs/4πεs(9)

which is reported but not drawn, and not applied to the current. As the doping rises the barrier thins and tunneling takes over. The crossover is governed by the characteristic energy

E00 = qħ2√N/m*εs   ≈ 18.5 meV × √(N/10¹⁸ cm⁻³)/[(m*/m0)εr](10)

giving thermionic emission for E00 ≲ 0.5 kT, thermionic-field emission up to ≈2 kT and field emission beyond (Padovani & Stratton). Rather than switching formulas, Fig. 5 computes all three regimes from one Tsu–Esaki integral,

J = A**Tk∫T(E) ln1 + e(EF,s − E)/kT1 + e(EF,m − E)/kT dE(11)

with a WKB transmission T(E) through the parabolic depletion-approximation barrier. The dashed curve is thermionic emission alone. The specific contact resistivity is ρc = (∂J/∂V)−1 at V = 0. In the thermionic limit ρc = (k/qA**T)eqφB/kT; in field emission ln ρc ∝ φB/√N. A series resistance from a 100 µm layer uses the Caughey–Thomas mobility,

μ(N) = μmin + μmax − μmin1 + (N/Nref)α(12)

with the published Si parameters and a generic fit for other materials.

6 · What the model leaves out

  • Interface layers (native oxide), whose thickness and charge make real diodes MIS structures with bias-dependent barrier heights and ideality n > 1.
  • Barrier inhomogeneity (Tung's patch model), which explains much of the measured temperature dependence of φB and n.
  • Minority-carrier injection, generation–recombination in the depletion region, and edge leakage.
  • Band-gap narrowing and band-tail states in degenerate semiconductors, and temperature dependence (all constants are at 300 K).

Model, equations and further reading

This tab joins two semi-infinite semiconductors, A on the left and B on the right, at x = 0. Each side's bulk statistics follow §2 of the metal–semiconductor tab: Fermi–Dirac carriers, incomplete ionization and the Mott transition. Degenerate layers such as ITO or n⁺⁺ Si are therefore handled correctly.

1 · Band alignment

Anderson's electron-affinity rule aligns the vacuum levels:

ΔEC = q(χA − χB),   ΔEV = (qχB + EgB) − (qχA + EgA)(1)

The rule ignores the interface dipole formed by charge rearrangement in the first few atomic layers, so measured affinities predict offsets poorly (Tersoff; Kroemer's Nobel lecture). The III–V affinities here are therefore effective values chosen to reproduce accepted offsets from Vurgaftman, Meyer & Ram-Mohan: Al0.3Ga0.7As/GaAs ΔEC ≈ 0.22, ΔEV ≈ 0.16 eV; InP/In0.53Ga0.47As 0.24/0.35 eV; InAs/GaSb overlapping by ≈0.2 eV. AlP (indirect, Eg ≈ 2.45 eV, nearly lattice-matched to Si) is placed with the same valence-band-offset scale, which puts its EV ≈ 0.94 eV below that of GaAs. Its dopant ionization energies are rough estimates, because AlP is little characterized: it is a key alloy end member (AlGaInP, AlGaP) but decomposes in moist air as a bulk material. Alignments are classified as Type I (straddling), Type II (staggered) or Type III (broken gap); the subtitle under the status pill names the type. Edit χ to try other offsets.

2 · Electrostatics

The exact first integral of Poisson's equation (Eq. 6 of the contact tab) is applied on each side from the interface into its bulk, giving ℰA(ψA) and ℰB(ψB) as functions of each side's band bending. The total bending equals the Fermi-level difference before contact, ψA + ψB = Vbi − V, and the split is fixed by Gauss's law at the interface,

εBℰB(0⁺) − εAℰA(0⁻) = σint(2)

where σint is an optional interface sheet charge (e.g. polarization charge at AlGaN/GaN). With σint = 0 and in the depletion approximation this reduces to the textbook result

xA = (2εAεBNB(Vbi − V)qNA(εANA + εBNB))1/2,   NAxA = NBxB(3)

which reduces further to the homojunction formula when εA = εB. The tab reports the depletion widths from Eq. (3) using the solved band bending, but the plotted profiles come from the exact integral. Accumulation layers, which Eq. (3) cannot describe, are therefore correct too.

3 · Current transport

Each carrier passes through two processes in series: thermionic emission over the highest point of its band edge (Richardson constant A** of the side it comes from), and minority-carrier diffusion into any side where it is a minority carrier. For a homojunction this reproduces the Shockley diode,

J = J0(eqV/kT − 1),   J0 = qni²(DnLnNA + DpLpND)(4)

with L = √(Dτ), capped at the 100 µm layer thickness (short-base limit). For heterojunctions it gives the injection asymmetry Jn/Jp ∝ eΔEg/kT behind wide-gap emitters, and spike-limited thermionic transport when a band-edge spike rises above both bulks. For p–n junctions, depletion-region recombination through mid-gap traps (Sah, Noyce & Shockley) is added,

Jgr ≈ qniW2τ(eqV/2kT − 1)(5)

which dominates at low forward bias in wide-gap and low-ni materials and produces ideality n ≈ 2. Lifetimes are typical values: 1 µs for Si and Ge, 10 ns for most III–Vs, 1 ns for nitrides.

4 · Band-to-band tunneling

When the junction field is large, electrons tunnel directly between the valence band on the p side and the conduction band on the n side. Kane's expression for a direct gap gives

JBTBT = q²ℰ√2mr4π³ħ²√Eg exp(−π√mr Eg3/22√2 qħℰ) · D(6)

where mr is the reduced effective mass, ℰ is the peak junction field, and D = ∫[fn(E) − fp(E)]dE is taken over the energies where the n-side conduction band overlaps the p-side valence band. D produces the Esaki peak and valley in degenerate p⁺⁺–n⁺⁺ junctions, and its growth in reverse bias gives Zener breakdown. If the n-side conduction-band minimum is indirect (Si, Ge, AlAs, SiC), the transition needs a phonon and Eq. (6) overestimates the rate by orders of magnitude; the model multiplies it by 10⁻² as a rough stand-in for phonon-assisted tunneling. ITO on p-Si is a direct (Γ-to-Γ) transition and keeps the full rate. Tunneling through heterojunction spikes is not modeled.

5 · What the model leaves out

  • Interface states and misfit dislocations. Lattice-mismatched pairs such as Ge/Si would have them, and they would pin the band alignment and add recombination.
  • Strain effects on band offsets and gaps.
  • High-level injection, band-gap narrowing in heavily doped regions, and trap-assisted (excess) tunneling current, which fills the Esaki valley in real diodes.
  • Quantization in accumulation notches. The heterostructure tab treats this with Schrödinger–Poisson.

Model, equations and further reading

The ideal MOS capacitor: a gate of work function φm, an insulator of permittivity κε0 and thickness tox with no leakage, and a semi-infinite semiconductor. Interface traps are absent. An optional fixed sheet charge Qf sits at the insulator/semiconductor interface. Semiconductor statistics follow §2 of the metal–semiconductor tab.

1 · Gate voltage balance

Going around the loop from the gate Fermi level to the substrate Fermi level,

VG = Φms + ψs − Qs(ψs) + QfCox,   Cox = κε0tox(1)

where Φms = φm − φs, ψs is the surface band bending (potential), and Qs is the semiconductor charge per unit area. Flat bands (ψs = 0, Qs = 0) occur at

VFB = Φms − Qf/Cox(2)

For a high-κ insulator the equivalent oxide thickness is EOT = tox(3.9/κ): the SiO2 thickness with the same Cox. Insulator band edges follow the electron-affinity rule; the oxide valence band is usually off scale.

2 · Semiconductor charge

Qs(ψs) comes from the exact first integral of Poisson's equation, Qs = −sgn(ψs)εsℰs(ψs). For a p-type substrate in the Boltzmann limit this is the classic Kingston–Neustadter result,

Qs = ∓√2 εskTqLD F(ψs),   F² = (e−βψ + βψ − 1) + n0p0(eβψ − βψ − 1)(3)

with β = q/kT and LD the extrinsic Debye length. The tab evaluates the Fermi–Dirac generalization of Eq. (3), with dopant ionization following the local band bending. The three regimes then follow from the sign and size of ψs: accumulation (ψs < 0 for p-type), depletion (0 < ψs < φB) and inversion (ψs > φB), where qφB = |EF − Ei| in the bulk.

3 · Threshold

Strong inversion is defined, as in most texts, by ψs = 2φB: the surface is as n-type as the bulk is p-type. In the depletion approximation,

VT = VFB + 2φB + √2εsqNA(2φB)Cox,   Wmax = (2εs(2φB)qNA)1/2(4)

The tab computes VT from the exact Qs(2φB), which agrees with Eq. (4) to within a few kT/q. Beyond threshold the inversion charge grows as Qinv ≈ −Cox(VG − VT) while ψs rises only logarithmically. Below threshold, Qinv grows exponentially with ψs: the weak-inversion regime that sets a MOSFET's subthreshold swing.

4 · Capacitance–voltage curves

The measured capacitance is Cox in series with the semiconductor capacitance:

1C = 1Cox + 1Cs,   Cs = |dQs/dψs|(5)

Low frequency: all charge, including the inversion layer, follows the AC signal, and C returns to Cox in strong inversion. High frequency: the DC bias point is the same, but minority carriers cannot be generated fast enough to follow the signal, so Cs includes only majority carriers and dopants. C then saturates at Cmin = [1/Cox + Wmax/εs]⁻¹. Deep depletion: the DC sweep is also too fast for an inversion layer to form, so W keeps growing past Wmax. That is the normal behavior of wide-gap materials (GaN, SiC), where thermal generation is negligible. The flat-band capacitance, CFB = [1/Cox + LD/εs]⁻¹, is the reference point for extracting VFB and hence Qf from measured data.

5 · What the model leaves out

  • Interface traps Dit(E): they stretch C–V curves, add frequency dispersion and are measured by the Terman, high–low and conductance methods. Also border traps and mobile ions.
  • Quantum confinement of the inversion and accumulation layers, which moves the charge centroid ≈1 nm away from the interface (adding ≈0.3–0.4 nm to the capacitance-equivalent thickness), and poly-gate depletion. Very thin oxides therefore look slightly better here than in reality.
  • Gate leakage (direct and Fowler–Nordheim tunneling) and dielectric breakdown.
  • The interfacial SiOx layer that every real high-κ stack contains.

Model, equations and further reading

This tab solves a layered structure along the growth direction z, from the surface (left) to the substrate (right). Depending on the stack it combines four solvers: Poisson's equation, the Schrödinger equation in a marked quantum region, transfer-matrix tunneling, and drift–diffusion for two-contact devices.

1 · Materials

III–V and III–N. Composition-dependent 300 K parameters for AlxGa1−xAs (Γ and X valleys; X is lowest for x > 0.45), pseudomorphic InxGa1−xAs on GaAs, and Ga-face AlxGa1−xN and InxGa1−xN on GaN, following Vurgaftman, Meyer & Ram-Mohan. Alignments use valence-band offsets: ΔEV = 0.53x eV for GaAs/AlAs, ΔEC = 0.65ΔEg for InGaAs/GaAs and 0.7ΔEg for the nitrides. Strain shifts of the gaps and the light-hole band are not included.

Polarization. Wurtzite nitrides carry a spontaneous polarization Psp and, when strained, a piezoelectric one Ppz. The bound charge is ρpol = −dP/dz, which concentrates into sheets at abrupt interfaces:

σpol = (Psp + Ppz)below − (Psp + Ppz)above(1)

Linear fits give σ/q ≈ 5.4×10¹³·x cm⁻² at AlGaN/GaN and ≈6.9×10¹³·x cm⁻² at InGaN/GaN (Bernardini, Fiorentini & Vanderbilt; Ambacher et al.).

Oxides, organics and metals. ITO is a degenerate n-type oxide (Eg 3.6 eV, m* 0.4, χeff 5.0 eV as deposited, giving φ ≈ 4.4 eV, or 5.3 eV after UV-ozone treatment, giving φ ≈ 4.7 eV). Organic layers use literature HOMO/LUMO energies with N0 = 10²¹ cm⁻³ molecular sites and single-level statistics, n = N0/(1 + e(ELUMO − EFn)/kT), which saturates at N0 instead of growing without bound. Metals are very heavily doped conductors defined by their work function. Dopants follow the Mott transition described on the contact tab.

Photovoltaic materials. c-Si (indirect, Eg 1.12 eV), a-Si:H (effective mobility gap 1.72 eV, fixed dopant activation energies of 0.25–0.3 eV, short lifetime; band tails and dangling bonds not resolved) and MAPbI3 (direct, 1.55 eV, εr ≈ 24). "ITO, ohmic tunnel contact" shifts that layer's bands so its Fermi level matches the neighboring semiconductor's: a flat-band contact. It stands in for tunneling through the thin depletion region at a real TCO/doped-a-Si:H contact.

2 · Electrostatics

ddz(ε(z)dφdz) = −q[p − n + ND+ − NA−] + dPdz(2)

Eq. (2) is discretized by finite volumes on a non-uniform mesh refined to 0.1 nm at every interface (geometric growth into thick layers) and solved by damped Newton iteration. Carriers use Fermi–Dirac statistics with incomplete ionization. The surface is either a Schottky gate (EC = EF,gate + qφB, which also models a pinned free surface) or an ohmic contact; the substrate end is a neutral ohmic contact.

3 · Quantum region: Schrödinger–Poisson

In the layers marked Q, electron states are found from the BenDaniel–Duke effective-mass equation on the Γ band,

−ħ²2ddz(1m*(z)dψdz) + EC(z)ψ = Eψ(3)

which enforces continuity of ψ and of (1/m*)dψ/dz at heterointerfaces. Hard walls at the region edges make the spectrum discrete. The matrix is solved by Sturm-sequence bisection plus inverse iteration. Each subband i holds a 2D sheet density

ni = m*kTπħ² ln(1 + e(EF − Ei)/kT)(4)

(the 2D density of states m*/πħ², including spin, integrated against the Fermi function), distributed as ni|ψi(z)|². Electrons above the region's lowest barrier edge are counted classically. Eqs. (2)–(4) are iterated to self-consistency with a predictor–corrector scheme and damping. Heavy-hole states are computed on the final potential but are not fed back into Poisson's equation.

4 · Tunneling transport

For unipolar structures (n–i–n), the transmission T(E) through the quantum region is computed with transfer matrices on the self-consistent potential, using the same BenDaniel–Duke matching. An adaptive grid resolves each resonance, however narrow. The current density is the Tsu–Esaki integral

J = qm*kT2π²ħ³∫T(Ez) ln1 + e(EF,L − Ez)/kT1 + e(EF,R − Ez)/kT dEz(5)

Transport is assumed coherent (no scattering), so resonances are too sharp and valley currents are underestimated.

5 · Bias in bipolar structures

In p–n structures (LEDs, lasers, the HBT at equilibrium) the electron and hole quasi-Fermi levels are taken as flat at their contact values. This is accurate when wide-gap claddings block minority leakage, and approximate for homojunctions. In n–i–n structures the quasi-Fermi level is interpolated linearly across the undoped region.

6 · Drift–diffusion for two-contact devices

When both outer layers are conductors (ITO, PEDOT:PSS or a metal), the layers between them are solved with the full semiconductor equations,

Jn = qμnndEFnq dz,   Jp = qμppdEFpq dz,   1qdJndz = R − G = −1qdJpdz(6)

coupled to Eq. (2) by Gummel's decoupled iteration. The continuity equations are written in Slotboom variables (w = eEFn/kT, v = e−EFp/kT) and discretized with Scharfetter–Gummel fluxes, with the band edge varying linearly within each element. Band offsets at heterointerfaces therefore need no special treatment. For molecular statistics a correction factor g = n/nBoltzmann is lagged by one iteration. Contacts are in thermionic equilibrium with the electrode, with image-force barrier lowering Δφ = (qℰ/4πε)1/2.

Recombination is R = Beff(np − ni²), with

Beff = Brad + CA(n + p) + 1τ(n + p + 2ni)(7)

combining radiative (or Langevin), Auger and mid-gap Shockley–Read–Hall terms (τn = τp = τ). In organic layers Brad is the Langevin coefficient γ = q(μn + μp)/ε: in a low-mobility solid, an electron and hole recombine as soon as diffusion brings them within the Coulomb capture radius. Inorganic lifetimes: 1 ms in c-Si, 10 ns in a-Si:H, 1 µs in MAPbI3. c-Si Auger: CA = 1.66×10⁻³⁰ cm⁶/s (ambipolar). The OLED recombination efficiency is ηr = q∫R dz / J.

7 · Optical generation

With illumination on, 1 sun enters at the surface. The photon flux in 0.02 eV bins is derived from the Shockley–Queisser short-circuit current JSQ(E) of the AM1.5G spectrum (≈43.8 mA/cm² above 1.12 eV; tabulated by Rühle). The intensity follows Beer–Lambert absorption layer by layer, with a 95 % specular second pass off a metal back mirror and ideal front anti-reflection:

G(z) = ΣE Φ(E) α(E,z) exp(−∫0zα dz′) + (back-reflected pass)(8)

Absorption edges are α ∝ (E − Eg)1/2 for direct gaps (MAPbI3, ITO, organics), ∝ (E − Eg)² for indirect c-Si (with a direct edge above 3.3 eV), and the Tauc form α ∝ (E − Eg)²/E for a-Si:H. Only pairs generated in inorganic semiconductors are collected; absorption in ITO and organic layers is a parasitic loss (organic excitons are not dissociated). The illuminated J–V gives Jsc, Voc, the fill factor FF = Pmax/(JscVoc) and the efficiency η = Pmax/(100 mW cm⁻²).

8 · What the model leaves out

  • Strain-dependent band structure, valence-band mixing (k·p), excitons and non-parabolicity in the quantum region.
  • Scattering in tunneling transport, and Γ–X mixing in AlAs barriers.
  • Gaussian disorder, field-dependent mobility, traps and exciton dynamics in organics; outcoupling.
  • Interface defect states, mobile ions and hysteresis in solar cells; texturing, reflection and photon recycling; temperature (300 K only).
